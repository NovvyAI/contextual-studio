# Short-drama analysis rubric

## Narrative beats

- **Opening state:** What is already wrong when the episode begins?
- **Immediate objective:** What does each principal character want in this scene?
- **Escalation:** Which arrival, accusation, discovery, deadline, or physical action increases pressure?
- **Decision:** What choice reveals character more clearly than dialogue does?
- **Reversal:** What changes the apparent meaning or power balance?
- **Reveal:** What fact becomes known to the audience, and which characters still do not know it?
- **Payoff:** Which line, gesture, or image resolves the episode's emotional argument?
- **Cliffhanger:** What unanswered consequence pulls viewers into the next episode?

## Character and relationship map

For each principal character, record:

- apparent role and evidence for it;
- immediate desire;
- fear or vulnerability;
- what they know versus what the audience knows;
- consequential action;
- gain or loss of agency;
- relationship position before and after the episode.

Do not promote inference to fact. Use labels such as “male lead,” “injured woman,” or “woman in blue” until names and relationships are established.

## Emotional curve

Describe audience emotion, not only actor expression. Useful categories include:

- curiosity;
- sympathy;
- romantic hope;
- unease;
- humiliation;
- anger at injustice;
- shock;
- vindication;
- relief;
- dread;
- anticipation of regret or revenge.

For each major beat, note stimulus → expected audience feeling → next-viewing impulse.

## Dialogue

Prioritize lines that:

- change a relationship;
- expose a mistaken belief;
- state a debt, promise, threat, or boundary;
- gain a second meaning after a reveal;
- can anchor a trailer or transition.

Preserve exact wording only when verified from subtitle, burned-in caption, or audible speech. Otherwise paraphrase and label it approximate.

## Visual and audio motifs

Capture:

- dominant palette and lighting;
- costume contrasts and status signals;
- recurring props;
- hands, doors, mirrors, beds, vehicles, letters, jewelry, wounds, food, or weapons;
- high-value gestures such as taking a hand, letting go, turning away, carrying someone, hiding evidence, or returning an object;
- framing patterns and camera distance;
- sound bridges such as heartbeat, door slam, dripping liquid, ringing, breath, or music drop.

For every reusable motif, document both its appearance and its story meaning.

## Contextual-ad handoff

Evaluate possible bridges on four dimensions:

1. **Emotional continuity:** Does the next action answer the feeling created by the episode?
2. **Visual continuity:** Can color, shape, motion, prop, composition, or sound transform naturally?
3. **Causal continuity:** Does the advertised interaction help the character pursue a plausible immediate goal?
4. **Product truth:** Does the interaction demonstrate the real product mechanic or a clearly framed narrative wrapper?

Prefer bridges where one action carries all four dimensions. Examples include a tear becoming water in a puzzle, a closed door becoming a game board, packing belongings becoming sorting, or repairing a room becoming a renovation loop. Reject transformations that trivialize injury, erase character agency, or contradict the episode's final choice.

## Evidence and confidence

Use these labels when ambiguity matters:

- **Observed:** directly visible or audible.
- **Strong inference:** supported by multiple cues, but not stated.
- **Speculative:** plausible interpretation requiring prior-episode context.

Call out gaps caused by sampling intervals, missing subtitles, inaudible audio, or lack of preceding episodes.
